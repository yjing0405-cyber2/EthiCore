Scenario 31: Trade Secrets and Corporate Disclosure
A senior engineer leaves a tech giant to join a rival startup, taking USB drives full of proprietary source code.
Decision A (Ethical): Destroy the drives before starting the new job; rely only on personal skills.
Immediate: The engineer must rebuild solutions from scratch at the startup.
Explanation: Respecting trade secrets means abandoning shortcuts and doing the hard intellectual labor again.
Ripple: The engineer's skills grow immensely.
Explanation: Building systems from the ground up forces the engineer to innovate rather than iterate on old ideas.
Long-Term: The startup releases an original product without fear of IP litigation.
Explanation: A clean codebase ensures the startup can seek funding and grow without a looming legal threat.
Decision B (Unethical): Use the stolen code to accelerate the startup's launch.
Immediate: The startup launches a competing product in record time.
Explanation: Stealing trade secrets acts as a massive, illegal catalyst for startup growth.
Ripple: The tech giant notices identical code structures in the startup's software.
Explanation: Large corporations heavily monitor rivals for exact replications of their unique technical architecture.
Long-Term: The tech giant sues, shuts down the startup, and the engineer goes to prison for corporate espionage.
Explanation: The theft of highly proprietary corporate data is a criminal offense that results in severe penal consequences.
Decision C (Mixed): Don't copy the code, but use the exact architectural blueprints memorized from the old job.
Immediate: Development is fast and technically original code is written.
Explanation: The engineer attempts to avoid copyright infringement by rewriting the code while still stealing the structural design.
Ripple: The product functions identically to the rival's.
Explanation: Because the underlying blueprint is the same, the software offers no real innovation.
Long-Term: The startup faces grueling legal battles over "clean room" design violations.
Explanation: Courts frequently rule that replicating complex architectural logic from memory still violates trade secret protections.
