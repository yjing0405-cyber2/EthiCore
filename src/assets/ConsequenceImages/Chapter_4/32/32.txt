Scenario 32: Product Misrepresentation and Caveat Emptor
A software publisher knows their new video game deletes player save files 10% of the time, but releases it anyway with a "No Refunds" policy.
Decision A (Ethical): Delay the game to fix the bug, or offer full refunds to affected players.
Immediate: The publisher loses money on development extensions or refunds.
Explanation: Taking responsibility for a flawed product requires absorbing the financial hit of fixing it.
Ripple: Players respect the publisher's transparency.
Explanation: Admitting fault and offering recourse builds tremendous goodwill and loyalty with the consumer base.
Long-Term: The game is eventually fixed, becoming a beloved, profitable franchise.
Explanation: Ethical treatment of consumers turns a rocky launch into a long-term commercial success.
Decision B (Unethical): Release it as-is and refuse all refunds under the guise of "Buyer Beware" (Caveat Emptor).
Immediate: The publisher secures initial sales revenue.
Explanation: The company abuses anti-consumer policies to lock in profits from a knowingly defective product.
Ripple: Furious gamers review-bomb the game and complain to consumer protection agencies.
Explanation: Consumers retaliate against fraudulent practices by destroying the product's public perception.
Long-Term: The publisher is heavily fined, banned from digital storefronts, and goes bankrupt.
Explanation: Modern platform holders will delist publishers who consistently scam users with broken software.
Decision C (Mixed): Release it, deny refunds, but promise a "future patch" to fix it eventually.
Immediate: The publisher keeps the money while buying time.
Explanation: This strategy strings the consumer along, hoping promises of future fixes will quell immediate anger.
Ripple: Players are annoyed but wait for the patch.
Explanation: The community enters a state of frustrated limbo, unable to play but unwilling to abandon the purchase entirely.
Long-Term: The game dies quickly due to negative word-of-mouth before the patch arrives.
Explanation: Promises cannot sustain a player base; releasing broken software usually kills the product's momentum permanently.
